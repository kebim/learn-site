CPU scheduling algorithms

When several processes are ready, the scheduler picks which one runs next. Good scheduling keeps the CPU busy and keeps users from waiting too long.

Key terms

Arrival time: when a process becomes ready. Burst time: CPU time it needs. Completion time (CT): when it finishes. Turnaround time (TAT) = CT - arrival. Waiting time (WT) = TAT - burst.

FCFS (first come, first served)

Run in arrival order. Simple, but one long job delays everything behind it (the convoy effect).

SJF (shortest job first)

Run the shortest burst next. It gives the lowest average waiting time, but long jobs can starve. The preemptive version is SRTF.

Round robin

Each process gets a time quantum, then goes to the back of the queue. Good response time; a tiny quantum wastes time on context switches, a huge one behaves like FCFS.

Example (all arrive at 0)

Bursts P1=6, P2=2, P3=4. FCFS order P1, P2, P3 gives waits 0, 6, 8, average 4.67. SJF order P2, P3, P1 gives waits 0, 2, 6, average 2.67.

Worked example: round robin

Processes P1=5, P2=3, P3=1, all arriving at 0, quantum 2. Order of execution: P1(2) P2(2) P3(1, finishes at 5) P1(2) P2(1, finishes at 8) P1(1, finishes at 9). Turnaround times are 9, 8 and 5; waiting times are 4, 5 and 4 (turnaround minus burst), average waiting 4.33.

Choosing an algorithm

Batch systems favour throughput (SJF). Interactive systems favour response time (round robin with a quantum of about 10 to 100 ms). Real systems such as Linux use priority-based schedulers that adjust to behaviour.

Practice

Three jobs with bursts 8, 4 and 2 arrive together. What is the average waiting time under FCFS in that order?

Show answer

Waits are 0, 8 and 12, so the average is 20 / 3 = 6.67. Under SJF (2, 4, 8) it is (0 + 2 + 6) / 3 = 2.67.
